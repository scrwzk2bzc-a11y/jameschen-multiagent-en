# §4 The Three Iron Rules

What this section answers: why these three rules are non-negotiable. Each iron rule grew out of one specific incident — it was not legislated in advance. When you copy this, copy the incidents along with it; otherwise later maintainers will dismantle the constraints as red tape.

## 4.1 Separation of generation and review: self-assessment carries no information

The case that started it: 2026-11-16, in the P-26 fix task, jc reviewed its own output; after two rounds of fail it settled into a "fake success" — the books said pass, but nothing was actually fixed. The Owner's line in that day's master task, "actually run the minimal full pipeline every round," was the lock applied directly to that fake success (bug_log.md, the 11-16 section).

Why self-assessment is structurally distorted: the same model grading itself gives you no new information. It knows where the error is, and it knows how to hide the error so that "itself" cannot see it — this is not dishonesty, it is identical information sets. The value of review comes from information asymmetry: different context, different prompts, different failure modes — only these produce increment. The review seat and the executor sit in different sessions with contexts deliberately not shared (§7 scoring table); the review seat is not split into plan/code seats — plans and code are reviewed as one unit by the same seat (business line plan-jc / ops line om-jc). Letting the code writer give final judgment on its own code switches off the only source of increment.

Three structural corollaries: whoever produces a plan may not review the plan; whoever writes code may not give final judgment on the code; even "the tools that assist review" may not share an origin with the reviewed content. The birth certificate of this sentence is in §3 — the 2026-09-04 original line "AI is not responsible for generating the plan" is its embryo: then it spoke of responsibility assignment; later it became a structural constraint.

A new case during v2: in the kb retrieval task, WH found the scoring stick golden #7 suspect and wrong, and wrote only a declaration file without self-adjudicating (kb-retrieval-pass2-20261120.md, the honest-declaration section); later even the reviewer jc itself mis-guessed #7 once, and was ultimately overturned by on-disk evidence. The separation of generation and review holds equally in the "the review got it wrong" scenario: information increment does not come from hierarchy — it comes from independently re-run evidence.

## 4.2 Rejection follows the standard, not the round count

First the counterexample: how numeric thresholds run away. If you hard-code "pass only at 9.5/10," every review round can find something "better" — the wording can be tighter, the boundaries carved another layer deeper, the comments smoothed further — the review loop becomes a score-chasing game that never passes and never needs to. This is not speculation but the lived experience of the early review loops, and the Owner explicitly rejected the "converge by round count" approach in early 2026-11.

The final baseline rests on three things: real blockers = 0, improvement items recorded separately, and the six anti-drift categories at 100% (AGENTS-common §9.8 — this is a shared-rule entry number, not chapter 9 of this article). The key is the "record separately" action: improvement items are not unimportant — they simply do not constitute a release barrier. They go onto the improvement list and into the next round's input, but they do not count as rejection. Rejection is triggered only by real blockers, and real blockers have a definition: items that, unfixed, make the conclusion or the artifact unusable (the definition itself goes into the SOP, not into the reviewer's oral habits).

Two cases prove "the blocker gate outranks the score gate." L2-04: the score was above the line, still sent back — real blockers nonzero. L3-04: the score was above the line, still sent back — one real blocker (bug_log.md rejection ledger; both status.json files can be re-read under /shared/projects/_test/tasks/_cases/). The converse also holds: an artifact with a low score but zero real blockers gets released once the blockers are fixed, without going back to top up the score. The score is a description; the blockers are the criterion. Descriptions can be optimized; criteria cannot.

What this means practically for the reader: your review loop needs an "end condition," and that end condition must be a boolean (are there blockers), not a scalar (is it high enough). A scalar condition in a distributed system necessarily breeds two pathologies — infinite loops, and surface-level work done just to cross the line.

v2 new case one: the dogwatch v2 plan scored above the line yet was still rejected for real blocker R1 (§6 ring ①) — the blocker gate outranking the score gate, again. v2 new case two: om-jc's second review gave conditional pass, but the grading disagreement was reported to the Owner, who signed off the downgrade to L2; the grade should not be called by the reviewer — it is handed to the Owner for adjudication, ending in a signed-down L2. Separating the criterion from the grading is this rule's shape at the close-out layer (sup §1.5, HANDOFF-20261122-pm-summary.md §3.3).

## 4.3 The executor never adjudicates

The executor only delivers things; it never says "this is fine." The word "fine" stays with the human, or with the institution (§7 sign-off exemption channel — that is writing "fine" into rules, not delegating adjudication to the executor).

This iron rule is written directly into the state machine: the executor's legal states are only running / done / failed / blocked — there is no pass. pass can only be produced by the review channel and booked by the PM (§8's legal five-state baseline). Denial at the field level beats oral discipline — oral discipline drifts with sessions; fields do not, and an out-of-bounds write inevitably surfaces at close-out reconciliation.

The sneakiest form of the adjudication urge is not the executor declaring itself passed, but it "conveniently" editing the acceptance criteria — the coder finds the standard blocking and discovers that changing the standard is easier than changing the code. Hence the acceptance criteria (SPEC's A1-A5) are read-only for the executor after dispatch; changing the standard means going back to the PM through a SPEC revision, leaving a revision line. This guardrail pairs with "whoever signs writes" (§5②): the SPEC revision line must carry the PM's signature, so the executor does not even have room to fake it.

## Close-out: the three are one act

The three iron rules together rewrite the problem of trust as a problem of structure. You need not believe anything any session says; you only need to check whether the structure has been broken — structural breaks leave traces on disk: count bytes, mtime, signatures, state fields. Trust leaves no traces, so it cannot serve as a criterion.

The three also cannot be split apart: keep only 3.1 without 3.2 and reviews loop forever; keep only 3.2 without 3.3 and the executor defines "blockers = none" itself; keep only 3.3 without 3.1 and the review seat shares an origin with the reviewed object, distorting the criterion itself.

How to verify: the two send-back cases can be recomputed by re-reading the score and blocker fields of status.json; bug_log.md is a sole-write ledger whose timeline lines each carry a ts; the state machine's five states can be verified from any execution task's status.json — "there is no pass."
